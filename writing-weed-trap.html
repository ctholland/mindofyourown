Systems & Democracy

The Weed Trap

What happens when millions of Americans have been taught that something is legal—and government quietly redraws the line?

C.T. Holland  ·  2026

For almost a decade, Americans have been receiving a fairly clear cultural message about cannabis: the war is ending. States legalized it. Stores opened. Products appeared everywhere. Billboards went up. Tax revenue rolled in. Cannabis moved from the back alley to the shopping district.

People adjusted. That matters, because once millions of people reorganize their behavior around something government has permitted, changing the legal boundary does something very different from prohibiting the behavior in the first place. It creates a population that may not realize the boundary moved. And right now, some of those boundaries are moving.

Congress changed the federal definition of hemp in 2025. The new standard counts total THC rather than simply delta-9 THC and creates a federal limit of 0.4 milligrams of covered THC per container for many finished hemp-derived cannabinoid products. The change was originally scheduled to take effect November 12, 2026; Congress has temporarily pushed most of the implementation to December 11.

That sounds like agricultural regulation. It isn't merely agricultural regulation.

Under federal law, the definition of hemp determines whether cannabis material sits outside the Controlled Substances Act or crosses back into federally controlled marijuana or THC territory. The distinction can therefore separate an ordinary commercial product from controlled-substance exposure.

Meanwhile, another strange legal split has emerged. In April, the Justice Department placed FDA-approved marijuana products and marijuana regulated under qualifying state medical licenses into Schedule III. But that order did not grant the same federal treatment to ordinary recreational marijuana. DOJ simultaneously continued the broader administrative proceeding over marijuana's federal scheduling.

So follow the architecture. Medical cannabis receives one federal lane. Recreational cannabis remains in another. Hemp products can move across the legal boundary based upon chemistry, source, formulation and packaging. States add their own rules governing quantity, potency, purchase location, public use and legal sourcing.

A person can therefore believe, quite reasonably, that they are participating in America's newly legal cannabis culture while still occupying a remarkably complicated legal landscape.

That is where my interest begins.

Because American history gives us a reason to pay very close attention whenever broadly practiced behavior becomes selectively enforceable. Cannabis enforcement has never fallen evenly across the population.

The ACLU's national study found Black Americans 3.64 times more likely than white Americans to be arrested for marijuana possession despite comparable usage rates. Legalization dramatically reduced arrests in many jurisdictions, but racial disparities persisted.

New York provides an especially instructive example. For years, possession of small amounts of marijuana had technically been decriminalized when kept private. But marijuana displayed in public remained arrestable. Police could order someone to empty their pockets, transforming private possession into public view. More than 80 percent of those arrested for marijuana possession in New York during the period examined were Black or Latino.

The statute didn't have to say arrest minorities.

The architecture did the sorting.

That distinction matters now. The dangerous power of a law does not necessarily come from prosecuting everyone who violates it. Sometimes its power comes from leaving enormous numbers of people technically exposed while deciding who gets noticed, who gets stopped, who gets searched, whose package gets examined, whose product came from the wrong source, who was using it in the wrong place, who possessed the wrong quantity, who is on probation, who has an immigration proceeding, who has something else the government wants.

That possibility becomes more consequential because something else is happening simultaneously. The United States is building an extraordinary amount of detention capacity.

The principal documented purpose is immigration enforcement—not cannabis enforcement—and those systems must not be casually conflated. But the 2025 reconciliation law supplied roughly $45 billion for immigration detention capacity and tens of billions more for ICE enforcement, while plans have contemplated capacity exceeding 100,000 detainees. This piece treats that as a separate system while asking the more important institutional question: what capacities are being constructed, and how might their uses evolve?

For noncitizens, the cannabis and immigration systems already have a bridge. Federal immigration policy can treat marijuana conduct as controlled-substance activity even where the conduct was legal under state law. Cannabis-related admissions, employment, arrests or convictions can therefore produce immigration consequences that American citizens do not face in the same way.

That should get our attention. It does not prove that somebody in Washington designed the hemp law as the opening move in a secret plan to sweep marijuana users into detention camps. I looked for that evidence. I haven't found it.

No identified directive. No quota. No procurement document. No interagency memorandum. No detention contract connecting these pieces into such a program.

But democracy does not require us to wait for the memorandum marked Sinister Plan before examining what government has made possible. Systems matter. Capacity matters. Discretion matters. History matters. And technical illegality matters enormously when enforcement is selective.

This is where the conversation about hemp becomes much larger than hemp. A society can spend years normalizing behavior, building an industry around it, collecting taxes from it, advertising it and allowing citizens to arrange their lives around it. Then the line moves.

Maybe the package is wrong. Maybe the THC concentration is wrong. Maybe the source is wrong. Maybe the location is wrong. Maybe federal law and state law disagree.

Suddenly government possesses something it had partially surrendered:

a reason to look.

That may be the civil-liberties issue hiding underneath the cannabis debate. Not whether every marijuana user is about to be arrested. They won't be. Selective systems do not require everyone to be arrested.

They require enough law to create discretion, enough discretion to choose targets, enough information to find them, and enough infrastructure to process the people selected.

The unresolved question is whether the current cannabis rollback remains primarily a regulatory battle over an unruly hemp marketplace—or becomes another piece of a much larger American reconstruction of enforcement power.

There is evidence for the first. There is reason to watch the second.

And history suggests that when government redraws the boundary between ordinary behavior and criminal behavior, the most important question is rarely how many people crossed the line.

It is who gets punished for crossing it.
