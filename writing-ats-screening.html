Applicant Screening: ATS and Institutional Neglect

How modern hiring technology fails job seekers—and the companies looking for talent.

C.T. Holland  ·  August 15, 2026

The modern hiring system has developed a recognition problem. Companies say they are looking for talent, judgment, adaptability, initiative, range, and people who can think beyond the job description. Then many of them rely on applicant tracking systems and automated screening tools built to search for resemblance: the same title, the same industry, the same software, the same keywords, and the same orderly progression from one nearly identical job to the next. That may make thousands of applications easier to process. It does not necessarily identify the person most capable of doing the work.

There is a difference between recognizing what someone has already done and recognizing what that person can learn, understand, solve, build, or become. Modern hiring technology is much better at the first task, yet too many companies behave as though it can perform the second.

An Applicant Tracking System, commonly called an ATS, can collect, organize, search, rank, and filter applications. Some platforms incorporate automated assessments, matching systems, recommendation engines, artificial intelligence, or other tools that influence which candidates advance. Not every ATS uses artificial intelligence, and not every automated hiring decision happens inside a single piece of software. The technology varies, but the experience on the receiving end is remarkably consistent.

A person submits an application after spending an hour revising a résumé, rewriting a cover letter, answering repetitive questions, reconstructing employment dates, and entering information already contained in the document they uploaded. Then the application enters a process they cannot see. They may receive an automated rejection within minutes, receive one weeks later, or hear nothing at all. They have no way of knowing whether a human being read the résumé, whether the software parsed it correctly, whether a missing phrase eliminated them, whether a previous title was judged insufficiently similar, or whether the system assigned meaning to something that had little to do with their ability to perform the job. In many cases, the company may not know either.

That is where administrative efficiency begins to become institutional neglect.

Most employers did not design the screening systems they use. They subscribed to them. They purchased access to a platform, selected certain functions, configured a workflow, connected it to their hiring process, and began relying on the results. That distinction matters, but it does not erase responsibility. An institution does not escape responsibility by subscribing to the system that shaped the decision.

The vendor builds the technology. The employer chooses it. Human resources establishes the process. Recruiters work from the resulting candidate pool, and hiring managers review the people who survived the earlier screens. The applicants who disappeared before that point remain invisible to everyone downstream. The employer can point to the vendor, the vendor can point to the employer's settings, the recruiter can say the résumé never reached them, and the hiring manager can say they reviewed every candidate they received. Everyone participated in the process, yet responsibility dissolves inside it.

This is one of the defining characteristics of automated institutional systems. The technology does not need to make the final decision to exercise power. It only needs to influence who becomes visible, who receives consideration, and who vanishes before a human judgment is ever made. A candidate who never reaches a person does not merely lose an interview. They lose the opportunity to be interpreted.

A résumé is not a person. It is a compressed record of selected experience, written for an unknown reader, submitted into a system operating by largely undisclosed criteria, and expected to carry the full burden of explaining someone's future value. That was already an imperfect proposition. Automated screening has made the compression more severe.

Many hiring systems are designed to identify correspondence. They ask whether the previous title matches the current title, whether the candidate has worked in the same industry, whether the résumé contains the expected terminology, whether the required platforms are named, and whether the career history resembles the profile the company already associates with success. Those questions are not meaningless. They are simply inadequate.

They privilege whatever can be easily compared. They reward direct repetition and favor the candidate whose past most closely resembles the company's present. They can punish reinvention, range, nonlinear careers, cross-disciplinary intelligence, unconventional experience, and people whose strongest value cannot be reduced to a familiar title. The system asks whether the person has already done precisely this. The better question is whether the person can understand this environment, identify its structure, learn what is necessary, exercise judgment, and begin generating value. Those are not the same question.

A person can have years of direct experience and still be unable to think beyond the procedures they inherited. Another person can enter an unfamiliar environment, understand how the parts relate, connect it to knowledge from other domains, ask better questions, and become effective quickly. The first candidate is easier for a screening system to recognize. The second may be more valuable.

This is where the familiar phrase "transferable skills" becomes too small. It sounds like carrying a familiar tool from one desk to another. It does not fully describe the ability to take knowledge, judgment, pattern recognition, learning capacity, and hard-earned experience from one field and produce useful performance in another.

I call that generative applicability.

Generative applicability is not merely the ability to repeat a known task in a new setting. It is the ability to enter a new context, recognize what matters, determine what can be transferred, learn what cannot, and generate an effective response. It is the capacity to distinguish surface difference from structural similarity, connect information that other people keep separated, and understand that experience is not valuable only when it can be copied directly from one job description into another.

It is also inseparable from generative learning capacity: the ability not merely to absorb information, but to reorganize it, connect it, test it, and convert it into useful performance. Some people know how to perform the work they have already been shown. Other people know how to enter a system, determine how it operates, identify what they need to learn, and become useful quickly. That difference is difficult to represent in a list of job titles and keywords, but it may determine who succeeds once the job begins.

My own background crosses executive support, medical research administration, real estate, operations, technology, design, media, music, writing, and the study of language and human behavior. A crude screening process could interpret that as inconsistency. A more intelligent evaluator might recognize repeated evidence of learning, synthesis, organization, judgment, communication, and the ability to become effective across different environments.

My background does not prove that I can do only what I have already done. It proves that I know how to learn, connect, adapt, and work across domains. That is not a claim that I am entitled to every job I pursue. It is a challenge to the logic by which applicants are increasingly screened. Previous sameness is not the only evidence of future capability. Sometimes it is not even the best evidence.

There is a legitimate grievance here, but it is not that every applicant deserves an interview, that qualifications should be abandoned, or that employers can make hiring decisions without standards, limits, or judgment. The grievance is that institutions increasingly rely on systems that may be unable to recognize important forms of human capability, then treat the system's inability to see those qualities as evidence that they are not there.

The person is not rejected after being understood. The person may be rejected because the process was incapable of understanding them.

That difference matters to older workers whose depth of experience may be translated into "overqualified," "too expensive," or "not current enough" before anyone speaks with them. It matters to people returning to work after caregiving, illness, economic disruption, or family obligations. It matters to candidates changing industries and to people whose careers developed before every responsibility was translated into the language currently rewarded by screening software. It matters to applicants with disabilities who may encounter inaccessible systems or assessments that measure the effects of a disability rather than the skills required for the job. It matters to anyone whose intelligence becomes more visible through conversation, judgment, synthesis, or action than through keyword correspondence.

This is not only a fairness problem for job seekers. It is a failure of institutional intelligence.

Companies complain that they cannot find qualified people while relying on systems that may be filtering qualified people out before anyone meets them. They publish job descriptions asking for adaptability, innovation, initiative, critical thinking, collaboration, creativity, and resilience, then build hiring funnels that reward familiarity. They say they want people who can operate through change, then favor candidates whose histories show the least deviation from an expected track. They say they want problem-solvers, then use systems that eliminate people whose experience requires interpretation. They say they value diverse perspectives, then screen for the closest available replica of what they already know.

This is how an institution can injure itself while believing it has improved efficiency.

The loss is difficult to measure because the rejected applicant disappears. There is no record of the judgment they might have brought, the connection they might have seen, or the problem they might have solved. No hiring report lists the candidates the system misunderstood. No quarterly review calculates the cost of people who were never considered because their experience did not resemble the preferred template. The company rarely knows what it missed because the process removed the evidence before anyone knew it existed.

Institutional neglect does not require a conspiracy, malicious executives, or a deliberate plan to exclude capable people. Neglect can be procedural. It can be purchased. It can be automated. It can emerge when an institution adopts a consequential system without preserving adequate human judgment, accountability, transparency, correction, or recourse.

The company does not need to design the limitation. It only needs to rely on it.

The neglect occurs when an organization accepts the convenience of automation without assuming responsibility for what the automation cannot recognize. It occurs when no one asks which qualified people are being excluded or whether the system is mistaking an unconventional history for a lack of capability. It occurs when an applicant has no meaningful way to correct a parsing error, explain a nonlinear career, request reconsideration, or learn whether a human being ever reviewed the application. It occurs when the efficiency of the institution is treated as more important than the accuracy of the judgment, and when an orderly result is mistaken for an intelligent one.

Order is not intelligence. Speed is not discernment. Consistency is not accuracy. Automation is not accountability.

Jennifer Freyd's work on institutional courage provides a useful point of connection. Institutional courage asks what it means for an institution to examine itself, seek truth, protect the people who depend on it, and act responsibly even when doing so carries inconvenience or cost. Applied to hiring technology, the question becomes direct: What would institutional courage require before an organization delegates consequential judgment about a human being to an automated system?

It would require employers to understand what their systems actually do rather than hiding behind the convenience of subscription. It would require meaningful human review where the technology is known to be limited, accessible processes, clear accommodation procedures, mechanisms for correcting errors, and serious examination of the people the system excludes—not only the candidates it advances. It would require companies to test more than whether a tool produces legally defensible numbers. They would also need to ask whether it relies on crude assumptions about relevance, experience, career continuity, language, or potential.

Most of all, institutional courage would require the organization to remain responsible for the judgment it has delegated. The vendor may own the platform, but the employer owns the decision to use it. The recruiter may not have seen the rejected applicant, but the institution designed a process in which that applicant could be rejected without being seen. Accountability cannot disappear merely because the mechanism has become technical.

The answer is not to eliminate technology from hiring. Companies receive enormous numbers of applications, recruiters have limited time, and administrative systems are necessary. Technology can organize information, reduce clerical burden, identify genuine minimum qualifications, and help people manage the work. What it cannot do is convert the management of an application into a complete understanding of the applicant.

An Applicant Tracking System may be able to identify correspondence. It may locate familiar terms, titles, credentials, and sequences. It cannot reliably determine the full value of a person it has never met, and it cannot be assumed to recognize the forms of intelligence that are least reducible to a predetermined match.

It can search for what has already existed. It may be considerably worse at recognizing who can create what comes next.

The question is not only whether a candidate has performed the exact job before. The question is whether that candidate possesses the intelligence, judgment, discipline, learning capacity, and generative applicability to perform it now. A system built to recognize resemblance may never ask that question.

A serious institution must.
